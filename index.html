How emission policy shapes automaker strategy

Objective: predict which vehicles US automakers will offer, and what they will earn, under different emissions standards, and determine when an MPEC formulation solves this market equilibrium faster than sequential iteration.

Timeline
Jan 2026 – present
Where
Carnegie Mellon University
Advisor
Prof. Jeremy Michalek
Methods
Game theory, MPEC, Scholtes relaxation, mixed logit
Tools
Python

IIntroduction

Emissions standards decide which vehicles automakers build, and that lineup decides how fast the US fleet decarbonizes. A standard that ignores how firms respond can raise costs without cutting emissions. Regulators therefore need models that predict strategic behavior across an entire market, not one firm at a time.

MMethods

  • Model the market as a Stackelberg game: the government leads by setting an emission constraint to maximize social welfare; profit-maximizing automakers follow by choosing vehicle offerings until they reach a Nash equilibrium.
  • Represent consumer demand with a mixed logit model.
  • Embed the firms' optimality conditions as equilibrium constraints in one optimization problem, an MPEC.
  • Apply the Scholtes relaxation to the complementarity constraints, which otherwise make the problem numerically intractable, and tighten it toward the exact solution.

RResults (in progress)

I am benchmarking the relaxed MPEC against sequential iterative optimization (SIO), the standard approach, in which each firm re-optimizes in turn until no firm changes its lineup. The comparison measures solve time and solution quality as the number of firms and vehicles grows.

Integrating consumer choice in TEMOA

Objective: integrate consumer choice into TEMOA, an open-source linear energy systems optimization model, so that its vehicle adoption forecasts reflect how households actually buy cars.

Timeline
2026 – present
Partners
Toyota Research Institute, University of Toronto, NC State
Faculty lead
Prof. Joe DeCarolis
Methods
Linear programming, disutility costs, willingness to pay
Tools
Python, TEMOA

IIntroduction

Energy systems models are used to plan decarbonization pathways. They choose the least-cost mix of technologies, so they assume buyers switch to electric vehicles the moment lifetime costs favor them. Real households also weigh range, charging access and vehicle size. Ignoring those preferences can make projected EV adoption too fast and too abrupt.

MMethods

I am implementing two approaches and comparing their effect on TEMOA's results:

  • Disutility costs from MA3T: non-monetary costs derived from Oak Ridge National Laboratory's MA3T vehicle choice model, which vary by geographic region and household characteristics.
  • Willingness to pay (WTP): values for vehicle purchase attributes drawn from the consumer choice literature, converted into costs TEMOA can optimize over.

Both approaches enter as cost terms, so TEMOA remains a linear program.

RResults (in progress)

The disutility cost approach is being coded into TEMOA's codebase, and the WTP values are being integrated with postdoctoral researchers at the University of Toronto. The next step compares adoption trajectories from baseline TEMOA against each choice-aware version.

Which models predict car sales? A systematic review

Objective: identify which demand models researchers used to estimate automotive sales in studies published in 2024–2025, and what questions those models answered.

Timeline
2026, three-month revision
Status
Under review, Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews
Authors
J. Vicente, C. Forsythe, K. Gillingham, J. Michalek, A. Patil, K. Whitefoot
Methods
Systematic literature review

IIntroduction

Demand models translate prices, policies and vehicle features into predicted sales. They underpin estimates of how fast electric vehicles will spread and which incentives work. The choice of model can change a study's policy conclusions, so researchers and agencies need to know which models the field relies on.

MMethods

  • Screened 1,197 papers published in 2024–2025 within a three-month revision window.
  • Sustained a pace of about 20 papers per day, reading each paper's abstract, methods and supplementary information.
  • Classified each study's demand model, from random utility models (RUMs) to statistical models, across the engineering and economics literature.

RResults

Random utility models remain the most popular choice for estimating automotive demand.

The reviewed studies used these models to answer research questions on vehicle adoption and to estimate consumer preference parameters.

Understanding the Flammability Limits of NH₃/CH₄ Blends Under Lean, Diluted Conditions

Objective: simulate how the fuel-air ratio of an ammonia-methane blend changes flame stability and NOx emissions in a radial swirl burner, and find the operating range that balances the two.

Timeline
February – May 2026
Where
Carnegie Mellon University
Team
Aditya Patil, Ben Hroblak
Methods
3D RANS, Eddy Dissipation Concept, 42-species mechanism
Tools
ANSYS Fluent

IIntroduction

Ammonia burns without producing carbon dioxide and can be made from renewable electricity, so it could decarbonize gas turbines. Pure ammonia burns slowly and unstably, so engineers blend it with methane. The blend burns hotter, however, and higher temperatures drive the formation of nitrogen oxides (NOx), toxic pollutants that cause smog.

MMethods

  • Modeled a 3D radial swirl burner feeding a 250 mm × 96 mm cylindrical chamber in ANSYS Fluent, meshed with about 1.2 million tetrahedral cells.
  • Used a steady, density-based RANS solver with the Eddy Dissipation Concept for turbulence-chemistry interaction and a 42-species kinetic mechanism.
  • Ran four equivalence ratios, Φ = 0.74, 0.85, 1.0 and 1.1, spanning lean, stoichiometric and fuel-rich mixtures.

RResults

  • Lean (Φ = 0.74): a fast jet (about 8.9 m/s) ran nearly the full chamber length and created hot spots above 1,800 K near the inlet, driving NO and NO₂ formation.
  • Toward stoichiometric: the jet stalled near the inlet and formed a central recirculation zone that anchored the flame and spread heat more evenly. Slow gas at the chamber floor stayed longer in hot regions, so NOx peaked at Φ = 1.0.
  • Fuel-rich (Φ = 1.1): lowest NOx of the four cases, because little free oxygen remained for the Zeldovich (thermal NOx) pathway.

DDiscussion

The swirl that stabilizes the flame also traps gas long enough to form NOx.

The chamber floor acted as the main NOx source, which suggests changing the chamber's length-to-diameter ratio or adding secondary air. The results come from steady RANS simulations without experimental validation, so they show trends rather than exact emission levels.
